Abstract

Language-based anomaly clip retrieval searches pre-segmented short clips whose content matches a natural-language description of a normal or anomalous event. Real-world collection faces substantial cost and privacy constraints, while existing benchmarks cover a limited range of anomalous event types. We introduce CAViR (Cross-modal Anomaly Video Retrieval), a synthetic video-text benchmark comprising 41,315 short clips (1.36M frames) across 30 normal activity types and 68 anomalous event types. Five captions per clip yield 206,575 video-text pairs.

To test whether the generated data is learnable and transferable, we introduce FLAME (Flow-guided Language-video Alignment with Mixture-of-Experts). Token-Aware Mixture-of-Experts (TAM) routes visual and textual tokens to specialized experts for fine-grained event representation. A Cross-modal Flow Predictor (CFP) provides auxiliary motion supervision to align language with short-term visual dynamics.

After dataset-specific fine-tuning, FLAME achieves T2V/V2T Recall@1 of 71.6%/87.3% on CAViR, 41.0%/39.7% on UCFCrime-AR, and 33.4%/33.8% on XD-Violence, the highest among the methods compared in the main evaluation. In synthetic-to-real transfer, training on CAViR alone improves UCFCrime-AR T2V Recall@1 from 22.4% to 33.8%, without training on real anomaly videos or further fine-tuning on the target dataset.

Task and Applications

Each CAViR candidate is a pre-segmented short clip containing one dominant normal or anomalous event. Evaluation measures clip-level semantic ranking in both text-to-video (T2V) and video-to-text (V2T) directions; it does not evaluate temporal boundary prediction within long videos. This setting supports fine-grained queries such as “multiple intruders breaking into a house at night,” with potential applications in incident investigation and workplace safety review.

Benchmark

Benchmark Construction

  1. Event descriptions. We collect descriptions from UCA, UCFCrime-AR and OOPS!, removing ambiguous OOPS! entries. To expand coverage, 23 additional anomaly categories from UBnormal and NTU RGB+D 120 are described using Qwen3, Seed-Thinking-v1.5 and DeepSeek-R1. Manual filtering retains 50 descriptions per added category. The complete collection contains 41,315 base descriptions: 16,145 normal and 25,170 anomalous.
  2. Short-clip generation. Wan2.1 generates one clip per base description, using FP8 quantization and TeaCache acceleration. Each clip has 33 frames at 15 fps (approximately 2.2 seconds) and a resolution of 480 × 832 pixels (height × width). The full benchmark contains approximately 1.36M frames.
  3. Multi-style re-captioning. Qwen3-VL adds four complementary captions to each base description, producing five captions per clip and 206,575 video-text pairs.
  4. Event categories. LLM-assisted labeling, K-means clustering and manual merging, renaming and verification consolidate the taxonomy into 30 normal activity categories and 68 anomalous event categories. A manual audit of 10% of the automatically assigned labels yields over 97% agreement with verified labels.

These automatic metrics provide partial evidence of data quality; they do not establish perceptual realism or the absence of a synthetic-to-real domain gap. Short clips do not capture the full context of long surveillance recordings, and generation with one model may underrepresent difficult lighting, motion blur, compression artifacts and complex camera motion. Synthesis reduces the need to record identifiable participants in dangerous situations, while concerns inherited from source descriptions and pretrained generators may remain.

Method: FLAME

FLAME combines token-dependent event representation with auxiliary motion supervision to learn correspondences between language and short-term visual dynamics.

  • Token-Aware Mixture-of-Experts (TAM). TAM is incorporated into the Swin-B vision encoder and the BERT text encoder. A learnable router selects one of three FFN experts for each visual or textual token (N = 3, k = 1). Frame embeddings include learnable temporal information and are averaged to form a video representation.
  • Cross-modal Flow Predictor (CFP). For matched video-text pairs, the last six BERT layers fuse text and sampled-frame features through cross-attention, using text tokens as queries and frame features as keys and values. A flow head predicts optical flow, supervised by an MSE loss against MEMFOF pseudo-ground-truth flow.
  • Joint training. Video-text contrastive learning, video-text matching and masked language modeling are optimized together with the flow loss, weighted by β = 0.2. The default input uses three 224 × 224 frames per video and up to 56 text tokens.
  • Retrieval at inference. The CFP branch is discarded. Video and text embeddings produce initial similarity rankings; the cross encoder and matching head re-rank the top 128 candidates for each query in either direction.

Results

T2V denotes text-to-video retrieval; V2T denotes video-to-text retrieval. Recall@K is reported as a percentage (higher is better), while median rank (MdR) and mean rank (MnR) are better when lower. We distinguish pretrained evaluation, dataset-specific fine-tuning and direct cross-dataset transfer.

Training, inference and computational efficiency

The default model is trained for five epochs using AdamW, batch size 20 and a learning rate linearly decayed from 2.5 × 10−5 to 2.5 × 10−6. CAViR training takes approximately 63.5 minutes on four RTX 3090 GPUs; UCFCrime-AR training takes approximately 12.5 minutes on one RTX 3090.

FLAME has 390.7M trainable parameters. Removing the training-only 155.8M flow-prediction component gives 234.9M parameters for full inference with re-ranking. On UCFCrime-AR, top-128 re-ranking increases T2V R@1 from 31.4% to 41.0%, while increasing query latency from 1.0 to 61.5 ms under the reported measurement protocol.
